Age at the award

Laureates keep getting older

Before 1950 the typical chemistry laureate was 50. Since 2001 the median is 68, and in the 2010s it was 72. One often-cited reason is the growing wait between a discovery and its prize.

The youngest winner, Frédéric Joliot, was 35 in 1935. The oldest, John B. Goodenough, was 97 in 2019.

Shared prizes

The solo laureate has all but vanished

Through the 1970s, most prizes went to a single person. Dan Shechtman's 2011 prize for quasicrystals is the most recent unshared one. Every prize since has gone to two or three people, the most the rules allow.

Fields

Biochemistry moved to the centre

Up to 1950, organic chemistry won the most prizes: 13 of 42. Since 1951, biochemistry has led, with 23 of 75, from protein structures to genome editing. By our grouping, nuclear chemistry has not won since radiocarbon dating in 1960.

Where the work was done

From Germany to the United States

Before 1940, German institutions took 18 of the 40 awards. Britain led in the 1950s and 1960s. The US has led every decade since the 1970s, with 88 awards in all. Of those, 29 went to people born outside the US.

Women laureates

Eight women in 125 years

Eight of the 198 laureates are women. After Dorothy Hodgkin in 1964, no woman won for 45 years. Four of the eight have won since 2018.

    Records and oddities

    1958 · 1980  /  2001 · 2022

    Twice in chemistry

    Frederick Sanger won for the structure of insulin and again for DNA sequencing. K. Barry Sharpless won for chiral catalysis and again for click chemistry.

    1903 physics · 1911 chemistry

    Two different sciences

    Marie Curie is still the only person with Nobel Prizes in two sciences.

    1954 chemistry · 1962 peace

    Two unshared prizes

    Linus Pauling won alone both times, for the chemical bond and for his campaign against nuclear weapons testing.

    1938 · 1939

    Barred from accepting

    The Nazi government forced Richard Kuhn and Adolf Butenandt to decline. Both received their diplomas and medals after the war, but not the prize money.

    1911 · 1935  /  2006

    Family prizes

    Marie Curie's daughter Irène Joliot-Curie shared the 1935 prize with her husband. Roger Kornberg (2006) is the son of Arthur Kornberg, a 1959 medicine laureate.

    1916 · 1917 · 1919 · 1924 · 1933 · 1940–42

    Eight empty years

    No chemistry prize was given in eight years, most of them during the two world wars. The money went back into the Nobel funds.

    Data

    Laureates, birth dates, birthplaces, institutions, prize shares and citations come from the Nobel Prize API (version 2.1, free to use under CC0), retrieved 3 October 2026.

    Age is counted on 10 December of the award year, the day of the ceremony. Countries use present-day names. A laureate's country is that of the first institution listed for them at the time of the award.

    Our choices

    The six fields, the symbols and the short labels are editorial. We assigned them from each prize's official citation. Many prizes sit between fields, so treat the field charts as a rough guide.

    Each laureate card includes the opening sentences of the laureate's English Wikipedia article, shortened to whole sentences, under CC BY-SA 4.0, with a link to the full article. Death years come from the Nobel Prize API.

    The 2026 announcement time comes from the official announcement schedule. The time given is the earliest the prize will be announced.
